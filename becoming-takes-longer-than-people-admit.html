Becoming Takes Longer Than People Admit

April 28, 2026 · 10 minute read

Arnie Rose

Follow About

Author of @youwereneverseries. Books about identity, human behavior, and inner life.

Becoming takes longer than people admit. Most people know this privately. Almost nobody says it out loud, because saying it out loud means sitting with the discomfort of how long you have actually been at it, and how little shows yet from the outside.

People are happy to talk about success once it is visible. The breakthrough. The moment things finally clicked. The version of the story that starts with struggle and ends with arrival. What they do not talk about, at least not while it is happening, is the long, formless period before the breakthrough. The years where nothing was clicking. Where the work was real but the results were not. That part of the story tends to get cut once there is a better ending to tell.

What Nobody Shares Honestly About Becoming

The gap between where you are and where you want to be looks, from the outside, like a gap in results. But what it usually is, underneath, is a gap in time. You have not yet reached the point where the work you have been doing starts to show. That does not mean the work has not been happening. It means you are still inside the part of the process that does not have anything visible to offer yet.

The years before the visible result are not wasted years. They are the years when the foundation is being built, when the patterns are being formed, when the way of thinking that will eventually produce something real is still being worked out. None of that shows on the outside while it is happening. It only becomes readable in retrospect, once enough of it has accumulated into something that other people can finally see and recognize as progress.

Why the Public Version of Every Timeline Is Shorter Than the Real One

Success stories that get shared publicly are almost always told after the person already knows how the story ends, which means they are edited after the fact to sound like they had direction all along. The parts that went nowhere get left out. The years of real uncertainty get compressed into a sentence. What you end up seeing is a version of the process that was never actually lived that cleanly, presented as though it were simply the normal way things go for people who try hard enough.

This creates a slow, ongoing distortion in how long people think things are supposed to take. You end up measuring yourself against a timeline that was never real. The ten thousand hours gets mentioned without the note that those hours often span a decade. The overnight success gets covered without the seven years that came before the night. You absorb a sense of pace that does not match reality, and then you use that pace to judge your own progress, and the judgment almost always lands the same way: you are behind. The problem is not your progress. The problem is the benchmark you are using, which was built from a version of other people's journeys that was heavily edited before it reached you.

Why Becoming Feels Slower the More You Care

There is something worth naming here: the more genuinely invested you are in becoming something real, the slower it tends to feel. Because you are paying closer attention. You notice the gap between where you are and where you want to be with more precision than someone who is less invested. The awareness of how far there still is to go comes with caring about the destination.

Someone who cares less notices less. They are not measuring the distance between where they are and where they want to be with the same level of attention. They are not catching every place where they fall short of what they are trying to build. That is not a virtue in them. It is just a lower level of investment. The discomfort you feel is proportional to how much you actually care about getting there, and that discomfort is not evidence that you are failing.

How to Tell If the Becoming Is Actually Working

Because there is rarely external proof early in the process, it helps to know what to actually look for instead of waiting for a result that hasn't arrived yet.

  • You respond differently to a situation that would have derailed you completely a year ago, even if the difference is small.
  • You can name something specific about how you think now that you couldn't have named before, evidence the internal architecture is actually shifting.
  • The old version of the problem you were trying to solve looks simpler in retrospect than it did while you were inside it, a sign your understanding has actually deepened.
  • You're less interested in proving the becoming to anyone else, and more interested in whether it's actually true.

None of these require a visible milestone to count as real evidence. Most of what's actually changing during this period produces no external signal until long after the internal shift already happened.

The Cost of Measuring Yourself Against the Edited Version

Most of the damage does not come from the slow timeline itself. It comes from comparing that slow, unedited, still-in-progress timeline against a finished, polished version of someone else's. You are comparing your whole messy, uncertain, ongoing middle to their clean, retrospective ending. Your year three to their year ten, presented as if it happened in two. The comparison is not just unfair in terms of outcomes. It is not even looking at the same kind of thing. One is a life being lived. The other is a story being told after the fact.

This comparison gets harder the more visible other people's timelines become. Every platform is a curated version of someone else's best moments, and those moments get absorbed as evidence of what a normal timeline looks like. A few honest questions about who you are actually measuring yourself against, and by what standard, can do more than most advice about how to stay patient.

Why the Becoming Itself Resists Being Rushed

There is a real reason becoming cannot simply be accelerated past a certain point, regardless of how much effort or intention goes into the process. What you are building is not a product that can be manufactured faster with more inputs. It is a person. And people change through repeated experience over time, not through a single decision or a concentrated burst of effort. The nervous system changes slowly. Patterns that were built over years of repetition do not get replaced in weeks. What feels like being stuck is often just the natural pace of the kind of change you are attempting, which is the kind that actually holds once it takes.

This is part of why the same advice, given to two different people at two different points in their process, can produce completely different results. The advice is not the variable. The readiness is. What that means in practice is that the work you are doing now, even when it appears to produce nothing, may be preparing the conditions for something that is not ready to take hold yet.

What Long-Term Growth Actually Looks Like

Take someone trying to become genuinely confident in their work, not performed confidence, the real kind that doesn't require an audience to hold.

Year 1. Almost entirely failure, by the standard they were judging themselves against. Old approval-seeking patterns win under any real pressure. They notice the failure each time and conclude nothing is working.

Year 2. Still mostly the same pattern, but the noticing happens faster. They catch themselves reverting in the moment instead of realizing it hours later. This feels like no progress at all, because the behavior hasn't changed yet, only the awareness of it.

Year 3. The reversions still happen but recovery is quicker each time. A bad pattern that used to take a week to shake off now takes a day. Nobody watching from the outside would call this progress. The visible behavior still looks inconsistent.

Year 5. The new pattern wins most of the time without conscious effort. What used to require a deliberate override now happens automatically. By this point it's no longer a technique being practiced. It has become how they operate, indistinguishable from a trait.

Nobody watching years two or three would have predicted year five. The visible evidence in the middle years looked almost identical to the visible evidence in year one: still inconsistent, still reverting under pressure, nothing that would make a satisfying before-and-after post. The actual change was happening entirely beneath that surface, in how quickly the recovery came each time, a metric nobody was tracking except the person living it.

How to Stay in the Process When Nothing Is Visible Yet

  • Stop measuring your progress by what is visible and start measuring it by what is real. Are you building something actual, or are you performing the appearance of building?
  • Extend your timeline. Not indefinitely, but honestly. Most meaningful things take two to three times as long as you originally planned.
  • Separate the comparison from the conclusion. The psychology of comparing yourself to others explains why measuring your unedited timeline against someone else's edited one was never a fair comparison to begin with.
  • Read Growth Is Not Always Visible for more on why the work that matters is often the work no one sees.
  • Read You Are Still Becoming, because the process is not over, and the version you are building is worth building.
  • Read Not Everyone Blooms in the Same Season for why a longer timeline was never evidence of a lesser outcome.

The Real Cost of Quitting in Year Four

The most expensive moment in any long process of becoming is usually not the beginning, when everything is clearly hard and nobody expects otherwise. It is the middle, when things are still hard but you have been at it long enough that you feel like they should not be anymore. That is when the doubt is loudest. That is when the gap between effort and visible result feels the most unfair. And that is usually when people stop, because they have run out of evidence that continuing is rational.

This is the point where the gap between effort and visible result is at its widest, and also the point where most people stop. Not because they failed at what they were trying to do. Because they ran out of patience and evidence at precisely the moment the process was about to produce something real. The cost of stopping here is high because the work that was already done does not disappear, but it also does not compound into anything if the process gets interrupted before it has had a real chance to produce a visible, meaningful result.

The years before the visible result are not the part of the story you skip past once you have arrived somewhere. They are the part that built the person who was capable of arriving. The result does not exist without them. The long, invisible part is not the cost of the outcome. It is the substance of it.

The book You Were Never Ordinary is for the person who has been putting in the work for years without the results to show for it. It is not about convincing you that your efforts will eventually pay off. It is about helping you understand why the direction matters as much as the effort, and what to change when the effort is real but the results are not arriving.

Common Questions

Why does becoming someone different take so long?

Because lasting change usually requires repetition, not a single insight. The behavior or belief needs to be practiced enough times that it stops requiring conscious effort, and that repetition takes real time regardless of how clear the initial realization felt.

Why do successful people make their timeline look shorter than it was?

Most success stories are told retrospectively, which tends to compress years of confusion, false starts, and invisible effort into a clean narrative. The actual timeline almost always included more uncertainty than the version that gets shared.

How do I know if I'm actually becoming who I want to be, even without visible proof?

Look at how you respond to situations that used to be difficult, not at external milestones. Quiet shifts in patience, judgment, and self-talk are real evidence of becoming, even when nothing external has changed yet.

Is it normal to feel like nothing is changing even when I'm putting in effort?

Yes. The timeline between effort and visible result is almost always longer than expected. Feeling like nothing is changing usually means the results haven't caught up to the work yet, not that the work isn't having an effect.
