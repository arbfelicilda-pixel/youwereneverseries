Some Journeys Take the Long Way Home

April 25, 2026 · 8 minute read

Arnie Rose

Follow About

Author of @youwereneverseries. Books about identity, human behavior, and inner life.

Some journeys take the long way home. Not because something went wrong, not because you made the wrong choices or missed the right window, but because the long way is the only route that builds what you actually need.

This is one of the hardest things to hold onto when you are in the middle of it. When the path keeps looping back. When what felt like a temporary side road has now been going for years. When you have arrived somewhere that looks nothing like what you pictured when you started. That is when the story you tell yourself about the journey tends to shift from patience to doubt, and the doubt is usually not about the destination. It is about whether you belong there at all.

The Moment People Usually Decide They Failed

Most people do not decide their journey has failed because of the journey itself. They decide it the moment someone else arrives somewhere first. A peer gets the promotion. A friend reaches the milestone. Someone younger publishes the book or starts the company or makes the move that you have been thinking about for years. That is the exact moment the length of your own road stops feeling like a process and starts feeling like evidence of something being wrong. The journey did not change. The comparison did. And the comparison was the thing that did the actual damage, not the road.

That is where the actual pain lives. Not in the length of the road itself, but in the exact moment it gets compared to someone else's shorter one. The road felt fine until you measured it against something. Once you made that comparison, the same distance that felt like progress started to feel like failure. The road did not get longer. The measurement changed.

Myth #1

A Longer Path Means You're Less Capable

This is the assumption sitting underneath most of the shame around taking longer: if you were capable enough, it would have happened faster. If you had made better choices, picked the right path, moved with more clarity, you would already be there. The longer the road, the more evidence it seems to provide for the idea that something is wrong with you specifically.

Truth

Length and ability are not as directly connected as this assumption suggests. Studies on long-term achievement consistently show that persistence and the ability to keep going through uncertainty and setbacks matter more than speed of arrival. Many of the people who built the most durable things took the longest to find their real direction. The assumption that faster means more capable simply does not hold up when you look honestly at how things actually unfold for most people across a full life.

Myth #2

People Who Succeeded Quickly Had a Cleaner Path

Success stories that get shared publicly tend to compress years of confusion and wrong turns into a clean, readable arc: struggle, breakthrough, arrival. The version you see is always the edited one. The lived version had more dead ends, more uncertainty, and more time where nothing was clear, but none of that makes the final version easier to tell.

Truth

Most success stories are told after the fact, which means they are shaped to sound coherent once the person already knows the ending. The parts that did not lead anywhere useful get cut. The years that felt pointless get reframed as character-building. What you end up with is a narrative that makes the journey sound like it had direction all along, when the person living it had no idea where they were going for most of it. That edited version is what you are comparing your unfinished, unedited life against.

Myth #3

A Long Journey Means You Wasted Time

This assumption treats time as the only thing that counts, as if a year spent going in the wrong direction produces nothing worth keeping simply because it did not lead to the intended destination. But direction and learning are not the same thing. You can go the wrong way and still come back with something real, and most people who took the long way have done exactly that.

Truth

Time spent on what looks like a detour is rarely wasted in the way it feels when you are inside it. Becoming the person who can actually do the thing you are working toward often requires exactly the kind of preparation that does not look like preparation at the time. It looks like delay. It looks like the wrong road entirely. And then, later, you can see that the wrong road was quietly building the capacity for the right one, but you can only ever see that clearly from the other side of it.

Myth #4

If the Long Way Were Right, It Wouldn't Feel This Hard

There is a common assumption that difficulty is a signal you are going the wrong way, that if the direction were right it would feel smoother. The right path should open up, not push back. When it is hard, something must be wrong with the choice or with the person making it.

Truth

Difficulty is not a reliable signal about whether you are heading the right way. Plenty of right paths are genuinely hard, and plenty of wrong ones feel smooth at first because they ask less of you. The hardness of a path tells you about the path, not about your fit for it. Some of the most right things a person can pursue are hard specifically because they matter, not because something has gone wrong.

Myth #5

Everyone Else Is Moving Faster Than Me

This is the assumption underneath the moment described earlier, the one that actually does the damage. The quiet certainty that if the journey is taking this long, it must mean you are not the kind of person who gets to arrive. That the length is proof, not just a fact about timing.

Truth

What you are seeing is other people's visible results, not the invisible costs behind them. You do not see what the milestone required them to give up, what it cost them privately, or what they are still working through. You see the arrival. You do not see the full road they were on to get there, and you certainly do not see what they left behind along the way to make that version of the destination possible.

What This Looks Like in an Actual Life

Take someone who spent eight years in a field that never quite fit, switching roles twice, going back to school once, restarting almost from scratch in their mid-thirties. From the outside, this reads as someone who couldn't decide what they wanted. From the inside, each one of those years was eliminating a wrong direction and building a specific skill that, on its own, looked unrelated to anything. By the time the actual path appeared, all of those years turned out to be exactly the preparation it required, even though nothing about the preparation looked deliberate while it was happening.

Nobody could have told that person, in year three or year six, that what felt like a detour was actually building something real. It only became visible from the other side, the way most long roads only make sense after you have finished walking them. That is how most long journeys work. The meaning arrives after the path, not alongside it.

Signs the Long Way Is Still Working

  • Skills or instincts from an earlier detour keep showing up useful in completely unrelated situations, years later.
  • You can name something specific you understand now that you couldn't have understood without the longer route.
  • The years that felt aimless at the time form a clearer pattern in hindsight than they ever did while you were living them.
  • You find yourself less rattled by setbacks than people who haven't had to navigate a long detour before.

None of these require the destination to have arrived yet. The process is not over just because it's taken longer than expected, and the signs above tend to show up well before any external proof catches up to confirm them.

What the Long Way Actually Builds

The version of you that took the long way is not behind. They are carrying more. They have seen more. They have built more of the kind of experience that does not show up on a list but shapes every decision that comes after it. The long road is not the consolation prize. It is a different kind of preparation, and what it builds tends to last longer and hold up better under pressure than what gets built quickly under easier conditions.

This is part of why comparing your chapter count to someone else's was never going to give you an accurate picture. You are not reading the same book. You do not have the same starting point, the same conditions, or the same destination. The only comparison that tells you anything useful is between where you are now and where you were, measured against your own history.

What to Do When the Long Way Still Feels Like Failure

When the long way feels like failure, the work is not convincing yourself it was secretly fine all along or that everything happened for a reason. The work is staying with the truth of it: this is hard, this has taken a long time, and you are still going. That is enough. You do not have to reframe it as a gift to keep moving through it.

  • Stop comparing your chapter count to someone else's. You don't know how many chapters they have left, or how many got cut from the version they're showing you.
  • Ask what the long way has built in you that the shortcut never could have.
  • Write down one specific thing you understand now that you didn't understand before the detour started. Make it concrete, not abstract. That specificity is the actual evidence the long way was doing something.
  • Read Not Everyone Blooms in the Same Season, because timing was never proof of failure.
  • Read Your Timeline Was Never Wrong, because the timeline you've been racing was never yours.
  • Read Becoming Takes Longer Than People Admit, because the real timeline for change is almost always longer than what gets shared.

The question was never why your road took longer. The question is why you decided longer meant failure.

If you are carrying the weight of a journey that has taken longer than you expected, You Were Never Behind was written for exactly this. It is a structural examination of why the pace you have been holding yourself to was never the right one, and what to do once you understand that.

This article references research on grit and achievement by Angela Duckworth, and studies on how success narratives are shaped retrospectively, often omitting the role of timing, luck, and circumstance in shaping how long a path actually takes.

Common Questions

Does taking longer to succeed mean I'm less capable?

No. The length of a path has little correlation with the ability of the person walking it. Many factors that determine timing, like circumstance, luck, and starting position, have nothing to do with capability.

Why do successful people make their path look easy?

Most success stories are told retrospectively, which tends to smooth out the false starts, luck, and detours into a cleaner narrative than what was actually lived. The mess rarely makes it into the version that gets shared.

Is a long journey a sign that something went wrong?

Not inherently. A long journey is simply a long one. Length alone says nothing about whether the journey was a failure or a success.

How do I stop feeling like my detours were wasted time?

Look at what the detour actually built in you, rather than how long it took. Skills, judgment, and resilience formed during a difficult detour are not erased just because the path wasn't straight.
